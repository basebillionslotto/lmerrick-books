The AI Boom: How a Few Corporations Captured the Chip

NVIDIA’s Rise, the Memory Wall, Photonics, and the Companies Trying to Break the GPU Lock

Ebook · $3.99 USD · L. Merrick

The generative-AI supercycle did not merely reward a clever graphics company. It concentrated physical and software power in a small set of public corporations: a GPU platform vendor that also owns the cluster fabric; a foundry that can print leading-edge logic and advanced packages; a Dutch lithography monopolist; three memory makers that can stack high-bandwidth DRAM; and a handful of hyperscale buyers whose capital budgets now look like industrial policy.

*The AI Boom: How a Few Corporations Captured the Chip* is a sourced, nonfiction account of how that concentration happened—and of the companies, technologies, and governments trying to loosen it.

L. Merrick traces NVIDIA from 1990s GeForce through CUDA (2006), the AlexNet moment (2012), Tensor Core GPUs (2017), the Mellanox networking acquisition (2020), and the Hopper-to-Blackwell-to-Rubin data-center platforms disclosed in NVIDIA’s own filings through fiscal 2026. The argument is not that NVIDIA “makes the best chip.” It is that CUDA, data-center GPUs, NVLink/InfiniBand/Ethernet networking, and an enterprise software stack together create a lock-in that competitors must attack as a system.

The book then widens the lens. TSMC and ASML set the physical tempo of the boom. SK hynix, Samsung, and Micron fight over HBM. CoWoS-class packaging and power and cooling—not just transistor density—decide who can ship. Microsoft, Google, Amazon, and Meta are not passive customers: they are designing custom accelerators (TPU, Trainium/Inferentia, Maia) while still buying GPUs at industrial scale. Photonics and co-packaged optics (Broadcom, TSMC COUPE, Ayar Labs) are real engineering programs, not science fiction—but pluggable optics still move most bits. U.S. Bureau of Industry and Security rules, published in the Federal Register since October 2022, have split the stack. Graphcore’s independent path ended in a SoftBank acquisition. Huawei’s Ascend line exists inside those controls.

This is not investment advice. It does not tell readers to buy or sell any security. Figures are dated to company reports, 10-Ks, and official notices available at the time of writing (August 2026). Where a 2026 number was not published or could not be corroborated, the book uses the last verified year and says so.

For readers of business, technology, and industrial history who want a clear map of the AI hardware oligopoly—without hype, without stock-chart theater, and without invented statistics.
